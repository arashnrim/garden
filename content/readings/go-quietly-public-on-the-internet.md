# Annotations

> To go quietly public online, you must first inhabit your world.

> When you inhabit your own world, you set your own norms of what is public and private. You don’t have to share your name, your face, your voice, your body, your family life, or your mental health. Or you could share all of those things, but choose to hide parts of your creative process.

> In contrast, being “loudly public” on the internet is showing your face, speaking to the camera, and sharing on multiple platforms as often as possible. Loudness online requires performance and catering to the audience.

> Social media isn’t antithetical to authenticity. The very performance of authenticity is expected in order to succeed on social media now.

> I imagine that an “authentic” me would not edit a caption over and over until it’s so blandly inoffensive that it means nothing. It would not have me trimming out all the pauses and ums and you knows from my Youtube videos.

> “I do wonder if tying the physical self to art incentivizes the wrong things. It places success largely in the hands of people who are willing to talk on camera, who can withstand people commenting on their looks, who can endure the stress of the weird and snarky commenters.””Do You Have to Become a Content Creator to Make It in the Arts?” by Hannah Glenn

> While I work on my craft of performance, I am mired in fear of rejection. This blocks my authenticity from shining, which affects what I end up sharing.

> But the tricky thing about social media is how quickly you can jump to the conclusion that the whole world is busily churning out carousels and think pieces and process shots. You start to think there is no other way to be online.

> Being quietly public means I mostly live in my internet home and my digital garden, where people can visit and get to know me beyond my mask.

> By being quietly public and going at my pace, my internet is alive. My internet is small. And I still have the capacity to love her.
