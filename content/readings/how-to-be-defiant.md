# Annotations

> I define defiance as acting in accordance with your true values when there is pressure to do otherwise.

> Defiance can be loud, but more often it’s quiet, considered and precise. It’s not about being difficult. It’s about being principled.

> My research has shown that a key reason defiance can be so hard, even when it’s clearly called for, is that we’re remarkably good at calculating the risks of defiance, but remarkably poor at calculating the risks of staying silent.

> In my book Defy: The Power of No in a World That Demands Yes (2025), I call this pattern ‘conscious compliance’: going along with something you disagree with, not because you’ve been fooled, but with full awareness that you’re choosing silence as a way to avoid discomfort in the moment.

> The short-term costs of defiance are immediate and visible: a difficult conversation, a moment of tension, perhaps a damaged relationship. In contrast, the costs of staying silent are delayed and diffuse: a growing sense of resentment, an eroding sense of self, the slow accumulation of a life shaped by other people’s expectations.

> People who habitually suppress their emotional expressions report less social support, feel less close to others, and experience lower social satisfaction. The strategy meant to preserve relationships actually undermines them.

> Each time it happens, briefly note: what did you stay silent about? What did you feel immediately afterward – relief, resentment, resignation? And what did you give up?

> One reason we default to silence is that we conflate discomfort with danger. The nervous flutter before a difficult conversation feels, to our bodies, remarkably similar to a genuine threat. But feeling unsafe and being unsafe are different things.

> Most situations we avoid are comfort issues masquerading as safety issues. Our nervous system, evolved to protect us from tribal exile and predators, doesn’t always calibrate well to the modern social stakes of the office or family dinner.

> The next time you’re weighing whether to defy, try this exercise: imagine you stay silent in this situation. What will it cost you in six months? A year? Five years? Be specific.

> Defiance is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with practice. If you’ve spent years defaulting to silence, the muscles of speaking up may be weak. Strengthen them gradually.

> What often holds people back, even in low-stakes moments, is not knowing what to say.

> These aren’t magic words. But having a few ready phrases that feel most natural to you lowers the barrier between the impulse to defy and the act of defying.

> Many people avoid speaking up because they associate it with conflict, selfishness or aggression. They imagine defiance as an attack on someone else. But remember: defiance is acting in accordance with your true values when there is pressure to do otherwise. Understood this way, defiance isn’t an attack. It’s an act of protection, of the values that make you who you are.
