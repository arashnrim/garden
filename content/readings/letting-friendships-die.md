# Annotations

> Here is what I don’t say. That scheduling these gatherings is my way of controlling the uncontrollable – the messiness of human connection.

> If I can slot friendship into Tuesday lunches and monthly dinners, I never have to face the terrifying openness of unstructured time, risk rejection or confront the possibility that, without these scaffolds, some relationships might simply not exist.

> I don’t know how to tell her that we haven’t really been friends for a long time. We’ve been two people who used to be close, going through the motions of closeness. Somewhere along the way, we outgrew each other – our interests diverged, our values shifted, the things that once connected us dissolved.

> Maybe when friendship requires constant work – when connection demands commitment rather than growth – something has already died. We just keep performing the rituals anyway.

> What I’m really afraid of, I realise, is the void. Of not knowing who I am without the constant performance of connection, or what’s left, without endless confirmations that I matter, that I’m needed, that I’m good at friendship. The relentless scheduling was holding my fear of being alone with myself at bay.

> Some friendships should die as we change and grow. I’m not meant to maintain every connection forever, while the guilt of letting relationships fade is often less painful than the slow suffocation of maintaining them.

> When I stopped propping up every connection, I discovered which ones had actual life – the ones that can handle silence and absence, that don’t require constant performance. Those are the friendships worth having.

> And it can survive the people we become – because we do become different people, and not every friendship is meant to make that crossing with us.
