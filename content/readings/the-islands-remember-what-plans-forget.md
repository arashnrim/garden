# Annotations

> Redevelopment has become Singapore’s background rhythm: steady and confident, even when the people who know these shores best are missing from the score. Most of the time, the logic is presented as common sense: land is scarce; the city must adapt.

> For a long time, these shores and islands weren’t leisure backdrops or future “assets.” They were places of work, dwelling, and kinship, known in detail by people who read the weather, the seasons, and the shifting ground as a matter of survival. Among them were Orang Pulau communities, whose lives were bound closely to the sea and the islands around Singapore’s coasts.

> Today, almost all Orang Pulau descendants live on the mainland. Relocation and reclamation moved families inland and reshaped the shoreline behind them. Some former island homes are now industrial zones; others were folded into tourism stories; still others, like Pulau Seletar, sit quietly on planning maps, waiting their turn.

> Islands rarely get presented as repositories of history and culture in their own right, or as nodes in older regional networks across the Nusantara. They show up as assets to be optimised, sorted into categories never built with them in mind.

> Coastal and Indigenous communities appear, if at all, as a brief nod to “earliest inhabitants.” Their knowledge of how these places flood, erode, and recover isn’t treated as central environmental data but as heritage material, documented after decisions have already been made.

> When these forests are reshaped, the impact isn’t only ecological. It touches a social landscape too: the estates where former islanders rebuilt the community, the routes between blocks and hill paths, the gathering spots that helped people adjust to life away from the sea.

> For Orang Pulau families and other coastal communities, islands were never simply “land parcels.” They’re where boats were launched, kin were visited, seasons were read; repositories of history, parts of a broader Nusantara world.

> None of this is to say development is, in itself, some kind of villain. Many of these families understand better than anyone that change is constant. The problem lies in the method and pace, and in the assumption that technical and economic logic alone are enough to chart the future of these places.
